## Lexical preferences
Prefer wording such as:
- "frequent utilization"
- "further deterioration"
- "significant demand"
- "vital in addressing"
- "implemented"
- "performed better"
- "achieved"
- "resulted in"
- "improved the overall results"
- "it is crucial"
- "we observed"
- "we believe"

Use these sparingly and naturally.
Do not force them into every section.

## Style boundaries
- Do not copy text from prior papers.
- Do not import prior citations or technical claims.
- Do not mimic an example paper's exact section structure if the new paper needs a different structure.
- Use this guide only for:
  - tone
  - sentence rhythm
  - paragraph flow
  - contribution phrasing
  - results narration
  - level of claim conservativeness

## Editing target
When revising prose using this style, aim for:
- formal and clear
- application-motivated
- technically grounded
- conservative in claims
- explicit in comparisons
- evidence-first in results

## Academic register refinements
- Keep the prose formal, objective, precise, and technical.
- Prefer evidence-led wording over conversational emphasis.
- Use grammatical complexity only when it improves precision or cohesion.

## Passive voice guidance
- Use passive constructions when the process, observation, or outcome matters more than the actor.
- Passive voice is especially appropriate for:
  - experiment descriptions
  - observations
  - dataset or metric definitions
  - generally accepted statements
- Do not overuse passive voice when active voice is clearer.

## Hedging guidance
- Use hedging to avoid sweeping statements and overclaiming.
- Prefer cautious verbs and phrases such as:
  - "suggests"
  - "appears to"
  - "is likely to"
  - "may"
  - "might"
  - "could"
  - "is considered"
  - "it is believed that"
- Use stronger, more direct claims only when the evidence is explicit and sufficient.

## Noun-phrase and nominalisation preference
- Prefer compact academic phrasing when it improves clarity, e.g. noun phrases and light nominalisation.
- Use nominalisation to make statements more formal and compact when appropriate.
- Do not let noun-heavy phrasing become difficult to read.

## Cohesion and cause-effect phrasing
- Prefer formal cause-effect phrasing such as:
  - "resulted in"
  - "led to"
  - "can be attributed to"
  - "stemmed from"
  - "because of"
  - "due to"
  - "as a result of"
- Prefer noun-phrase complements after expressions like "because of", "due to", and "as a result of".

## Transition-phrase discipline
To keep prose natural and less templated:
- avoid repeated use of generic connective phrases such as "in this context," "in this setting," "overall," and "therefore"
- do not insert a transition phrase unless it improves clarity
- prefer sentence-to-sentence logical flow over visible rhetorical scaffolding
- vary connective phrasing across adjacent paragraphs
- if a generic bridge phrase appears once in a section, strongly prefer a different construction the next time

## Relative-clause and modifier style
- Use defining and non-defining relative clauses to compress information cleanly.
- In formal passages, prefer fronted-preposition forms where natural, e.g.:
  - "in which"
  - "for which"
  - "with which"
- Reduced relative clauses and participial phrases are welcome when they make the sentence more concise without harming clarity.

## Sentence-density guidance
- Dense academic sentences are acceptable, but clarity comes first.
- Prefer structured complexity over clutter:
  - one core claim,
  - followed by qualification,
  - then evidence or implication.
